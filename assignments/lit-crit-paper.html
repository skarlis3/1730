Literary Criticism Paper: “Argue with a Character”

Overview

Characters don’t always see clearly. They misjudge other people, explain away their own choices, and tell themselves stories that the rest of the text quietly contradicts. For this paper, you’ll choose one reading from this semester, find a claim a character makes, and argue that the text shows something different.

The Task

  1. Choose a reading. Pick any text we’ve read this semester that has at least one character who speaks or thinks on the page. The narrator counts if they are also a character in the story.

  2. Find a character’s claim. Look for a moment where a character states a judgment or explanation. It might be about another character, an event, or themselves. Good claims often sound like:

    • a judgment of someone else (“He never cared about anyone but himself.”)
    • an explanation of their own actions (“I only did it to protect her.”)
    • a description of a situation (“Nothing has changed here in years.”)
    • a prediction or belief (“Everything will be fine once we leave.”)

  3. Argue with it. Write a thesis arguing that the text shows something different from what the character claims. You might argue the claim is wrong, only partly true, or true in a way the character doesn’t realize.

    • Weak: “Tim says he isn’t jealous of his brother, but he is.”
    • Stronger: “Tim insists he isn’t jealous of his brother, but his constant comparisons and his habit of changing the subject whenever his brother succeeds suggest that his denial is a way of hiding resentment even from himself.”

  4. Use the text as your evidence. Support your argument with specific details: other characters’ words, the character’s actions, the narrator’s descriptions, imagery, word choice, or contradictions between what the character says at different moments.

  5. Explain why the gap matters. In your conclusion, explain what the difference between the character’s claim and the text’s evidence reveals about the character or the work as a whole.

What Strong Papers Do

What a strong paper does compared with common pitfalls
A strong paper… Instead of…
Argues what the text reveals Arguing whether the character is a good or bad person
Builds its case from specific details in the text Relying on plot summary or general impressions
Explains how each piece of evidence contradicts or complicates the claim Quoting and assuming the contradiction is obvious
Considers why the character might believe or say what they do Simply calling the character a liar
Chooses a claim with something at stake Picking a minor or obviously false statement
Ends with what the gap shows about the character or work Restating the thesis

A useful test: if your paper could be summed up as “the character is wrong,” push further. Ask why they see it that way, and what the text gains by letting them.

Getting Started

  1. Skim the reading and mark every place a character judges, explains, or describes something. Dialogue and inner thoughts are both fair game.
  2. For each claim you marked, ask: does the text back this up, or does something push against it?
  3. Pick the claim with the most evidence against it, or the most interesting gap. A claim that’s only partly wrong often makes the best paper.
  4. Gather evidence. Look at what the character does, what others say about them or the situation, what the narrator notices, and anything the character contradicts later.
  5. Ask why. Why might the character believe this, or say it out loud? Your answer to this question often becomes your thesis.

Suggested Structure for Two Pages

  • Introduction: briefly set up the text and character, quote the claim, and end with your thesis.
  • Body (2–3 paragraphs): each paragraph presents one kind of evidence that complicates the claim, with analysis.
  • Conclusion: explain what the gap between the claim and the evidence reveals about the character or the work.
