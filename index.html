What this course is

Throughout the semester, we'll read a range of recent American literature and consider questions like: What are people reading? What is literature? Who decides that? And what shifts might be happening in the literary landscape? You'll read an award-winning novel made popular by BookTok, short stories published in literary magazines, a humorous and poignant graphic novel, a fanfiction short story, and even interactive storytelling on Reddit.

Every one of those routes runs through gatekeepers: editors, publishing companies, prize juries, librarians, algorithms, moderators, a tagging system, a comment section. The question we keep coming back to isn't which of these is good. It's what people are reading in the 21st century, what gets counted as literature, and what each of those systems lets through, keeps out, and rewards.

That question isn't settled — and in this class we're keeping it unsettled on purpose.

Before you read the fanfiction

Reading a fic for class is not the same as being in that fic's community. The author is a private person who wrote something for free and did not sign up to be coursework. Don't comment on the fic as a student, don't tag the author, and don't bring the classroom into the archive.

How we'll read

Week eight you get three critical lenses, and for the rest of the semester you pick one and use it. Three is the point: choosing becomes a decision instead of a default.

I

Close reading

Meaning lives in the text. You were already doing this in high school; it just had a name.

II

Reader response

Meaning lives in the reader. BookTok is reader response at industrial scale; the nosleep comments are readers completing the text.

III

New historicism

Meaning lives in the world. What surrounds the text, who published it, what it was answering.

If you leave with a firmer definition of literature than you walked in with, something has gone wrong. The goal is a better argument, held more carefully, with more behind it.
